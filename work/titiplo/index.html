TITIPLO

TITIPLO started from a simple question I kept coming back to: why does a jastip business still have to run almost everything from chat? I wanted to see how far I could take that idea.

PlatformSeller App + Buyer Storefront

01 — THE PROBLEM

The idea started from something very familiar: jastip works because people trust the seller.

But once orders grow, the seller ends up juggling chat, spreadsheets, screenshots, payment proof, product availability, and status updates. I wanted to know whether that could feel more organized without removing the personal nature of jastip.

FRICTION 01

Scattered order context

Important information can live across multiple conversations and manual records.

FRICTION 02

Manual status updates

Seller and buyer visibility can depend on repeated chat updates instead of a shared flow.

FRICTION 03

Hard to scale trust

A public-facing product needs clearer structure, status, and transaction evidence than a close-circle jastip workflow.

02 — PRODUCT PRINCIPLE

I never wanted TITIPLO to feel like a marketplace.

The seller is still the center of the relationship. The product is there to make the boring operational parts easier, while giving buyers a clearer way to browse, order, pay, and follow what is happening.

PRINCIPLE

Mobile first

The seller workflow is designed around a phone, because the business happens while sellers are moving, sourcing, and communicating.

PRINCIPLE

Minimal infrastructure

Use lightweight serverless components to keep the product inexpensive to operate and simple to evolve.

PRINCIPLE

Guided workflow

Status, actions, and navigation should reduce the amount of business logic the seller has to remember manually.

03 — ARCHITECTURE

I also wanted to keep the infrastructure cheap enough to make sense for a small product.

That pushed me toward Cloudflare Workers and D1, with Google Drive handling payment proof files. It is not a fancy architecture on purpose; I wanted something I could understand, maintain, and afford.

04 — CORE FLOWS

The seller and buyer needed different experiences.

The seller needs control and speed. The buyer mostly needs clarity and confidence. So I stopped trying to make one interface do both jobs.

Seller flow

1
Create trip

Define destination, currency, schedule, and selling context.

2
Add products

Manage pricing, markup, images, and availability per trip.

3
Manage orders

Track customers, order items, proof of payment, and seller actions.

4
Purchase & handoff

Move the transaction through purchase and shipping or hand-carry completion.

Buyer flow

1
Browse storefront

Discover available trip products from a public buyer-facing experience.

2
Place order

Submit item selection with clearer structure than a chat-only request.

3
Submit payment proof

Attach transaction evidence to the same order context.

4
Follow progress

See transaction updates without relying entirely on manual seller messages.

05 — ITERATION

Most of the useful improvements came after something annoyed me during testing.

Back-button behavior, slow loading states, unclear buttons, notification destinations, password recovery, subscription history — a lot of the work has been small things that only become obvious once the product is actually used.

Seller workflow foundation

Trips, products, customers, orders, purchase, shipping, and file-proof handling.

Buyer storefront

A separate buyer-facing experience connected to the seller's operational data.

Identity & account layer

Login, registration, OTP verification, password reset, seller account settings, and session behavior.

Subscription layer

Free-plan logic, upgrade flows, subscription history, and manual-payment-ready infrastructure.

Release polish

Navigation behavior, loading feedback, sticky headers, button interaction states, and removal of internal technical messaging from the user experience.

OUTCOME

It stopped being a mockup a long time ago.

For me, that is the most interesting part of TITIPLO. It forced me to think about the whole product: what the seller sees, what the buyer trusts, what the backend stores, what breaks on a phone, and what it costs to keep running.
